Cargo Link Company Limited & Cargo Link Petroleum Logistics Company Limited
Profile 1.7
Summary Assessment
Cargo Link Company Limited (CLC) and its operational subsidiary Cargo Link Petroleum Logistics Company Limited (CLPL) were the junta's primary domestic distributors of aviation fuel between at least October 2021 and August 2022. Using a fleet of tanker trucks under contract to NEPAS, CLPL delivered 37.8 million litres to NEPAS airport storage facilities and 17 million litres directly to Myanmar Air Force bases, for a combined 54.8 million litres. Amnesty International linked four of the bases receiving fuel to attacks amounting to war crimes. Both entities are owned through May Thwe Aung, an Asia Sun Group-affiliated individual who has not been sanctioned in any jurisdiction. CLC is sanctioned by the UK and Canada. CLPL is sanctioned by the US, Canada, and Australia. Neither has been designated by the EU. The asymmetric coverage across the two entities creates a gap that the EU, UK, and US have not closed.

1
Role in the Aviation Fuel Supply Chain

CLC is the holding company and CLPL is its operational subsidiary. CLPL carried out deliveries under a NEPAS transportation contract that predated the coup by four years, originally as Cargo Link Pongrawe Logistics Company Limited, a joint venture with Thai company Pongrawe Co. Ltd. On 24 June 2022, CLC bought out Pongrawe's stake and renamed the entity Cargo Link Petroleum Logistics Company Limited. Since then it has been wholly owned by CLC and through it by May Thwe Aung. (AI Nov 2022)

Amnesty International documented CLPL's operations through 54 invoices covering October 2021 to January 2022 and three truck trip datasets covering December 2021 to August 2022. Between those dates CLPL delivered 37,837,844 litres to NEPAS airport storage facilities and 17,024,044 litres directly to Myanmar Air Force bases. It also made deliveries to and from the Mann Refinery, from which fuel was distributed onward to Meikhtila, Hmawbi, Nay Pyi Taw, Taungoo, and Monywa air bases. Approximately 50 percent of the documented truck fleet was used for military base deliveries, approximately 50 percent for airport storage facilities, and approximately 15 percent for both. Amnesty International authenticated the datasets through licence plate verification against Myanmar Car Checker, photographs on the company's public Facebook page, and corroborating testimony from two former Myanmar Air Force defectors. (AI Nov 2022)

Of the five air bases from which military aircraft involved in attacks departed between March 2021 and August 2022, Amnesty International linked four directly to attacks amounting to war crimes: Hmawbi, Magway, Tada-U, and Taungoo. Five airports in Myanmar formally share airstrips with military bases (Heho, Mandalay, Myeik, Nay Pyi Taw, and Yangon), meaning fuel delivered to NEPAS storage at those airports cannot be meaningfully segregated between civilian and military use. (AI Nov 2022)

2
Key Relationships
•Asia Sun Group Company Limited / Shoon Group: Ultimate parent network of both entities. CLC is owned by May Thwe Aung, an Asia Sun Group-affiliated individual, with Asia Sun Group confirmed as the ultimate controlling network per the UNSR's June 2024 Figure 9 sanctions network diagram. Asia Sun Group is sanctioned in all five jurisdictions. See Profile 1.4. (UNSR Jun 2024)
•May Thwe Aung: Beneficial owner of Cargo Link Company Limited (100%), per the Asia Sun Group entity table in Deadly Cargo. May Thwe Aung is identified in Deadly Cargo footnote 224 as one of three key individuals in their 30s who were among the central players in the Asia Sun Group alongside Win Kyaw Aung and Myo Myint Aung. She has not been sanctioned in any jurisdiction and does not appear on any known sanctions list. Her unsanctioned status means the individual ultimately controlling CLC and, through it, CLPL faces no targeted financial measures. (AI Nov 2022)
•Asia Sun Logistics Co. Ltd.: A further Asia Sun Group-affiliated entity whose trucks were used to service NEPAS storage facility deliveries. Amnesty International found that trucks used for NEPAS airport deliveries were almost all owned or managed by Cargo Link Pongrawe or Asia Sun Logistics Co. Ltd. This establishes Asia Sun Logistics as an operational participant in the distribution network alongside CLPL. (AI Nov 2022)
•National Energy Prime Aviation Services Company Limited (NEPAS): Primary client of CLPL for tanker truck distribution services. NEPAS paid CLPL for deliveries to NEPAS storage facilities at commercial airports. The 54 invoices document NEPAS as the paying party for airport deliveries. Amnesty International noted that the Myanmar military also appears to have contracted CLPL separately for air base deliveries. NEPAS is unsanctioned in all jurisdictions. See Profile 1.2. (AI Nov 2022)
•Myanma Petrochemical Enterprise (MPE): State-owned enterprise that controls the downstream aviation fuel sector in Myanmar. CLPL's deliveries to and from the military-controlled Mann Refinery formed part of the documented distribution network. MPE is unsanctioned in all jurisdictions. See Profile 1.1. (AI Nov 2022)
•Mann Refinery (Mann Thanbayarkan Petrochemical Complex): Military-controlled refinery that served as a storage facility for Jet A-1. NEPAS paid CLPL for truck deliveries to and from Mann Refinery. Documents reviewed by Amnesty International show that the military then distributed fuel from Mann Refinery to Meikhtila, Hmawbi, Nay Pyi Taw, Taungoo and Monywa air bases. See Profile 1.8. (AI Nov 2022)
•Puma Energy Asia Sun Company Limited (PEAS) / Myan Oil Terminal Company Limited: The Thilawa port terminal from which CLPL collected aviation fuel for onward distribution by tanker truck. At the time of the documented operations, the terminal was operated by PEAS. It was subsequently renamed Myan Oil Terminal Company Limited following Puma Energy's exit. See Profile 1.3. (AI Nov 2022)

The UK sanctioned CLC on 31 January 2023 on the basis that it had been and was involved in making available economic resources, specifically aviation fuel, directly or indirectly to or for the benefit of the Myanmar security forces. The US sanctioned CLPL on 24 March 2023 for involvement in the importation, storage and distribution of jet fuel to the Myanmar military. Canada sanctioned CLC in its January 2023 round and CLPL in its October 2023 round. Australia sanctioned CLPL in its February 2024 round. (UK Jan 2023; JFM Mar 2023; JFM Nov 2023; UNSR Jun 2024)

Sanctions gap: Canada has designated both CLC (January 2023) and CLPL (October 2023), though in separate rounds. No other jurisdiction has designated both entities. The UK designated CLC but not CLPL. The US and Australia designated CLPL but not CLC. The EU has designated neither entity, despite having sanctioned Asia Sun Group's parent structure and other network members. Coordinated designation of CLPL by the UK, of CLC by the US and Australia, and of both entities by the EU would close these structural gaps and ensure that no jurisdiction holds only half the corporate structure within its sanctions perimeter. (UNSR Jun 2024; AI Nov 2022)

Advocacy Interventions to Date
•November 2022. Amnesty International's Deadly Cargo and Justice for Myanmar's companion target list identify CLPL as the primary domestic distributor of aviation fuel and call for its designation. (AI Nov 2022; JFM Nov 2022)
•31 January 2023. The UK and Canada designate Cargo Link Company Limited (the parent). CLPL is not designated in this round. (UK Jan 2023)
•24 March 2023. The US designates Cargo Link Petroleum Logistics Company Limited for involvement in the importation, storage, and distribution of jet fuel to the Myanmar military. CLC is not designated by the US in this round. (OFAC Mar 2023)
•October 2023. Canada designates CLPL. Justice for Myanmar confirms the former name Cargo Link Pongrawe Logistics Company Limited and describes CLPL as the junta's main transporter of jet fuel to military bases and airports. (JFM Nov 2023)
•February 2024. Australia designates CLPL. Neither CLC nor CLPL has been designated by the EU as of April 2026. (UNSR Jun 2024)
5
Recommended Actions
•Designate May Thwe Aung individually in all jurisdictions. She is the 100 percent beneficial owner of CLC and through it the indirect owner of CLPL, identified by Amnesty International as one of the three key Asia Sun Group players. Her complete absence from any sanctions list, despite owning the entities that controlled the junta's primary aviation fuel distributor, is a significant individual accountability gap. (AI Nov 2022)
•Designate both CLC and CLPL in the EU. The EU has sanctioned Asia Sun Group but not either Cargo Link entity. The Amnesty International evidence package and the OFAC and UK designations provide direct multilateral precedent. (AI Nov 2022; OFAC Mar 2023; UK Jan 2023)
•Close the asymmetric designation gap: designate CLPL in the UK and CLC in the US and Australia. The UK designated the parent but not the operating subsidiary. The US and Australia designated the subsidiary but not the parent. Each jurisdiction has half the corporate structure outside its sanctions perimeter. CLPL as the operational entity falls within the UK's designation basis for CLC. CLC as the 50 percent-plus parent falls within OFAC's automatic designation rule. (AI Nov 2022; UK Jan 2023; OFAC Mar 2023)
•Verify current operational status. The documented operations cover October 2021 to August 2022. No subsequent source confirms whether CLPL continues to operate under the same name. Given the pattern of renaming across the Asia Sun / Shoon Group network, sanctions authorities should investigate whether CLPL remains active or whether a successor entity has assumed its role. (AI Nov 2022; UNSR Jun 2024)
