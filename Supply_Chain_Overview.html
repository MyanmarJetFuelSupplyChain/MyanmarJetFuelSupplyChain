Overview of the Post-Coup Myanmar Jet Fuel Supply Chain

Background

On 1 February 2021, Myanmar's military seized power and established the State Administration Council (SAC). The SAC has since conducted a sustained air campaign against civilian populations, targeting hospitals, schools, religious buildings, and displacement camps. That air campaign is entirely dependent on a continuous supply of Jet A-1 aviation fuel from international sources.

Myanmar's Air Force does not use a separately formulated military fuel. Jet A-1 is the same product used by commercial airlines worldwide. Former Air Force officers and leaked procurement documents confirm the Myanmar Air Force purchases and burns Jet A-1 for the vast majority of its aircraft. At the point of delivery to the Thilawa terminal in Yangon, a consignment bound for a Myanmar Air Force aircraft is physically identical to one bound for a commercial airliner. There is no practical mechanism, short of end-use monitoring after delivery, that distinguishes the two. Every litre entering Myanmar through the documented supply chain is available to the military.

The primary documentary record consists of five Amnesty International investigations published in November 2022, March 2023, January 2024, July 2024, and January 2026. The March 2023 report was published jointly with Global Witness and Burma Campaign UK. The UN Special Rapporteur on the situation of human rights in Myanmar published a dedicated report on the supply chain's financial architecture in June 2024. Reuters published an investigation drawing on satellite imagery and shipping documents in January 2026. Justice for Myanmar has produced supplementary reporting throughout.

The supply chain has never been interrupted. Each round of exposure and sanctions has produced a structural adaptation that maintained or increased supply volumes. Imports in 2025 reached 109,604 metric tonnes, the highest recorded figure since the coup and approximately 69 percent above 2024 levels. Myanmar's Air Force conducted 1,022 airstrikes on civilian targets between October 2024 and December 2025 alone, more than double the number from the preceding fifteen months, according to Myanmar Peace Monitor data. At least 1,728 civilians were killed in those attacks.

The Thilawa Terminal

Every documented international aviation fuel shipment to Myanmar since the coup has been offloaded at a single physical location, the petroleum products terminal at Thilawa port, approximately 16 kilometres southeast of Yangon. This terminal is the sole documented entry point for imported Jet A-1.

Puma Energy, a multinational majority-owned by Trafigura, built and operated the terminal from 2017 until December 2022. Puma ran the terminal through a subsidiary, Puma Energy Asia Sun Company Limited (PEAS), and held a 49 percent stake in the aviation fuel joint venture National Energy Puma Aviation Services Co. Ltd. (NEPAS), which managed imports and distribution across eleven Myanmar airports. The controlling 51 percent stake in NEPAS was held by the junta's Myanma Petrochemical Enterprise (MPE). Armed soldiers entered and occupied the terminal on the morning of the coup. Puma Energy resumed operations under military pressure, commissioned a confidential Human Rights Impact Assessment in early 2022 that concluded military access to fuel could not be reliably prevented, and announced its exit on 5 October 2022. The asset sale was completed in December 2022.

The buyer was Shoon Energy Pte. Ltd., a Singapore-registered company formerly called Asia Sun Aviation Pte. Ltd. and controlled by Khin Phyu Win. In April 2023, Khin Phyu Win transferred ownership of eleven Asia Sun network companies to Zaw Min Tun, a move the UN Special Rapporteur assessed occurred in anticipation of sanctions action. Both individuals have been sanctioned by the US, UK, and Canada. The terminal operating company has since been renamed twice, currently operating as Myan Oil Terminal Company Limited. The Singapore holding company behind the terminal structure, P.E.I. Energy Pte. Ltd. (formerly Puma Energy Irrawaddy Pte. Ltd.), has been sanctioned by the United States. The terminal operating company itself has not been sanctioned by any government. Shoon Energy and its successor jointly manage the terminal with MPE, and every subsequent international aviation fuel shipment has been offloaded there.

Domestic Distribution

Once fuel is offloaded at Thilawa, three entities manage distribution within Myanmar.

NEPAS, now rebranded National Energy Prime Aviation Services Co. Ltd. following Puma Energy's departure, holds the formal aviation fuel import licence and acts as the official purchaser of all Jet A-1 entering the country. It manages storage and refuelling at eleven airports. Between December 2021 and August 2022, Amnesty International documented NEPAS distributing over 54 million litres of aviation fuel, of which more than 17 million litres were delivered directly to ten Myanmar Air Force bases, including Taungoo (4.7 million litres), Nay Pyi Taw (4.6 million litres), Hmawbi (3.2 million litres), and Meikhtila (2.3 million litres). NEPAS has not been sanctioned by any government.

Cargo Link Petroleum Logistics Company Limited (CLPL), part of the Asia Sun Group network, operates the tanker truck fleet moving fuel from Thilawa to airports and military air bases. Amnesty International obtained records covering more than 1,500 truck trips between December 2021 and August 2022, approximately half to NEPAS airport storage facilities and approximately half to military air bases directly. Two former Myanmar Air Force defectors confirmed that fuel stored at NEPAS facilities was also transported onward to additional bases by military vehicles. Amnesty International linked four bases receiving fuel through this network to attacks amounting to war crimes. CLPL has been sanctioned by the United States, Canada, and Australia.

The Mann Thanbayarkan Petrochemical Complex in Magway Region, operated by MPE, supplements imported fuel with domestic Jet A-1 production. Situated between Magway Air Force Base and a military munitions facility, it occupies a location Justice for Myanmar described as highly strategic for the military. Leaked documents confirmed military trucks transporting Jet A-1 from Mann to multiple air bases. Industry expert analysis of night-time light emissions, cited in a Justice for Myanmar investigation, showed significant production increases at Mann from 2021 to mid-2023, consistent with junta statistics recording domestic aviation turbine fuel output at 174 percent above the same period a year earlier.

Phase One: Direct Commercial Supply (February 2021 to December 2022)

During the first twenty-two months following the coup, Jet A-1 was delivered to Myanmar through standard commercial channels. Fuel was loaded at named refineries in Singapore, Thailand, and India and shipped directly to Thilawa. Amnesty International's November 2022 Deadly Cargo investigation documented eight shipments, with two further shipments documented in the March 2023 follow-up report co-published with Global Witness and Burma Campaign UK, bringing the Phase 1 total to ten.

Four of the eight Deadly Cargo shipments loaded at the Singapore Refining Company (SRC) terminal on Jurong Island, a joint venture between Chevron Singapore Pte. Ltd. and Singapore Petroleum Company Ltd. (SPC), a wholly-owned subsidiary of PetroChina. For the December 2021 SPC shipment of 20,270 metric tonnes aboard the Synergy, Amnesty International obtained a leaked Myanmar Ministry of Defence letter to the Myanma Port Authority confirming the fuel was required for use in "Tatmadaw (Air Force)'s Aircrafts and Helicopters." This document establishes explicit military end-use on the face of the official import record. Chevron confirmed its own February 2022 shipment of 13,310 metric tonnes aboard the Super Hero. ExxonMobil's adjacent Jurong Island berth is implicated in the June 2022 Super Infinity shipment of 13,311 metric tonnes; ExxonMobil made no commitment to cease future sales. Thai Oil Public Company Limited confirmed supply of 4,942 metric tonnes from its Sriracha terminal in June 2022. Bangchak Corporation supplied 12,592 metric tonnes from Bangkok Port in October 2022. Rosneft supplied 15,710 metric tonnes loaded at the Vadinar Oil Terminal in Gujarat, India, in December 2021.

Due diligence across all suppliers was limited to sanctions-list screening and written warranties from Singapore intermediaries that fuel was for commercial use only.

Following Amnesty International's pre-publication engagement, Thai Oil suspended Myanmar aviation fuel sales on 14 October 2022. Wilhelmsen Ships Service Myanmar, the port agent handling fuel vessel arrivals at Thilawa, issued a cessation statement on 18 October 2022. Pan Ocean Co. Ltd., whose vessels carried three documented shipments, committed after publication that its ships would no longer transport aviation fuel to Myanmar. None of the three has been identified in any subsequent documented shipment. Chevron and ExxonMobil made no equivalent commitment.

Phase Two: Vietnamese Transit (April 2023 to mid-2024)

Following Deadly Cargo and the first rounds of coordinated sanctions by the US, UK, EU, Canada, and Australia, the SAC restructured its procurement to insert a Vietnamese transit point between international fuel suppliers and the Thilawa terminal. The purpose was to allow sellers earlier in the chain to claim ignorance of the fuel's ultimate destination.

The mechanism was as follows. An original fuel supplier sold Jet A-1 to a Singapore trading company. That trader sold the fuel, sometimes through more than one intermediate transaction, to a Vietnamese company. The Vietnamese company received and stored the fuel at the Cai Mep Petroleum terminal near Ho Chi Minh City, operated by Hai Linh Co. Ltd. After storage at Cai Mep, sometimes for only a few hours, the fuel was sold onward to Myanmar and shipped directly from Vietnam to Thilawa. Amnesty International documented seven shipments using this model in 2023 and two in early 2024, totalling at least 67,000 metric tonnes in 2023 alone.

The primary delivery vessel throughout the bulk of this phase was HUITONG 78 (IMO 9646479), a Chinese-flagged tanker. The West of England Protection and Indemnity Club assessed that HUITONG 78 is owned by an affiliate of China Aerospace Science and Industry Corp (CASIC), a Chinese state-owned defence company, and stated that no insurance coverage applied to those trades. YIDA 8 (IMO 9936941) carried the April 2023 shipment.

Vietnamese customs data enabled Amnesty International to identify three Singapore-registered traders supplying the Vietnamese transit companies. BB Energy (Asia) Pte. Ltd. was the second-to-last seller in at least three of the seven 2023 shipments. Sahara Energy International Pte. Ltd. supplied the January 2024 shipment valued at approximately USD 8 million, with fuel traceable to the Vopak Singapore Banyan Terminal before resale. CNOOC Trading (Singapore) Pte. Ltd. supplied the February 2024 shipment, also approximately USD 8 million, with fuel traced to the BP Hua Dong terminal in Ningbo, China. None of the three responded to Amnesty International. None has been sanctioned. Hai Linh Co. Ltd. has not been sanctioned, and Vietnam has taken no regulatory action against it.

The supply chain paused twice during this phase. It stalled between January and March 2023 following the Deadly Cargo publication, and again between September and November 2023 following the August 2023 OFAC jet fuel sector determination. The supply chain resumed after each pause.

Phase Three: Iranian Ghost Fleet (mid-2024 to present)

From mid-2024, the SAC's aviation fuel supply shifted to Iranian-linked vessels deploying sanctions evasion techniques drawn from Iran's shadow fleet operations. This transition introduced a state-level actor into the supply chain and produced a marked increase in delivery volumes.

Reuters confirmed in January 2026, using SynMax Intelligence satellite imagery and shipping documents, that fuel in this phase originates from Bandar Abbas, Iran, from a refinery operated by the National Iranian Oil Refining and Distribution Company (NIORDC), a subsidiary of the National Iranian Oil Company (NIOC). OFAC designated NIOC as an agent or affiliate of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) in 2012. Reuters estimated Iran earned approximately USD 123 million from nine shipments to Myanmar between October 2024 and December 2025. In January 2022, an Iranian government delegation secretly visited Myanmar to discuss weapons sales including guided missiles, according to a regional security source reported by Asia Times. In December 2025, Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian met Myanmar's Prime Minister Nyo Saw on the sidelines of a Turkmenistan summit; the Iranian readout noted Myanmar's desire to expand cooperation on oil imports and extraction.

Vessels in this phase employ four documented evasion techniques. They disable AIS transponders during loading and offloading, broadcast false position data while under way, repeatedly change vessel name, flag, and ownership, and conduct fuel transfers at sea to avoid port records. The Fujairah Offshore Anchorage Area (FOAA) off the UAE coast serves as the primary staging point before the final leg to Myanmar, providing a legitimate-appearing waypoint.

The two primary vessels are REEF (IMO 9263382) and NOBLE (IMO 9162928). REEF, formerly PETRA and then BALTIC HORIZON, is a Guinea-flagged tanker confirmed in four shipments between October 2024 and December 2025. Satellite imagery confirmed it loading at Bandar Abbas on at least one documented occasion, with shipping documents linking it to Bandar Abbas for further deliveries. A BlackSky satellite image confirmed REEF docked at the Myan Oil Terminal on 9 December 2025. The AIS spoofing on the September 2025 loading was documented with precision. REEF's transponder placed it near Iraq's Basrah Oil Terminal while satellite imagery simultaneously confirmed it at Bandar Abbas. Three minutes after the AIS showed the vessel approximately 330 kilometres from the port, the signal indicated arrival at that port, a transit that would require a minimum of ten hours. The day after REEF's December 2025 delivery, a SAC airstrike on the 300-bed hospital in Mrauk-U, Rakhine State, killed at least 30 people and injured more than 70. NOBLE, formerly HURON, then TRAA3, then ASTRA, is a Guinea-flagged tanker confirmed in four deliveries between January and June 2025. Both vessels were designated by the United States in 2024 for transporting sanctioned Iranian petrochemical products, before their role in Myanmar's aviation fuel supply was publicly documented. Reuters reported REEF and NOBLE together delivered approximately 175,000 metric tonnes of jet fuel to Myanmar across nine shipments between October 2024 and December 2025.

REEF is owned by Amethyst Maritime Ventures SA and managed by Sea Route Ship Management FZE, both registered in the UAE's Hamriyah Free Zone. NOBLE is owned by Sea Hill Maritime SA, registered at the same address, and also managed by Sea Route Ship Management FZE. HUITONG 78, the primary Phase 2 vessel, continued operations into Phase 3 and was subsequently renamed BARAAWE 1 following public exposure. YONG SHENG 56 (IMO 9657507) carried one documented shipment in July 2024, staging at the Fujairah Offshore Anchorage Area in the UAE before delivery, and was subsequently renamed LS MERCURY.

Financial Flows

The UN Special Rapporteur's June 2024 Banking on the Death Trade report documented the payment architecture for calendar year 2023, when approximately USD 80 million in aviation fuel purchases flowed through this network, approximately 30 percent above 2022 levels.

All documented payments originated from Myanmar-side entities and cleared through United Amara Bank (UAB), a private Myanmar bank. UAB processed payments from NEPAS directly and from Myanmar-registered proxy companies established to obscure the purchasing entities, among them Intraco (subsequently renamed International Green Agrotrade Company Limited, established on behalf of NEPAS on 20 September 2023), Swan Energy Company Limited, Myan-Oil Company Limited, and Rich Ray Trading Company Limited. The UN Special Rapporteur recommended that international financial institutions freeze relationships with UAB. UAB has not been sanctioned in any jurisdiction.

Payments from UAB arrived at accounts held by two Thailand-registered trading companies whose beneficial ownership, registered address, and corporate registration details do not appear in any publicly available source. CB Energy received approximately USD 40 million from NEPAS directly and approximately USD 15 million from Asia Sun Group proxy companies, all into accounts at Krung Thai Bank. Tawan-Oil Trading received approximately USD 25 million from the NEPAS proxy Intraco and a portion of approximately USD 15 million from Asia Sun Group proxy Rich Ray Trading, all into accounts at Kasikorn Bank. The UN Special Rapporteur found that a basic review of Myanmar corporate records by either bank would have revealed the purchasers' connection to Myanmar's aviation fuel trade. Swan Energy's own publicly accessible website stated it supplied 10,000 metric tonnes of aviation fuel per month to Myanmar, representing 100 percent of Myanmar's aviation fuel market. Neither CB Energy nor Tawan-Oil Trading has been sanctioned in any jurisdiction.

Following US designation of Myanma Foreign Trade Bank (MFTB) and Myanma Investment and Commercial Bank (MICB) in June 2023, the SAC shifted broader procurement banking to Myanma Economic Bank (MEB). The UN Special Rapporteur documented MEB processing over USD 60 million in military procurement payments in the second half of 2023 alone. MEB has not been sanctioned by the US, UK, EU, or Australia, but has been sanctioned by Canada.

Domestic Crude Supply

The junta supplements imported aviation fuel with domestically produced Jet A-1, refined at the Mann complex from crude oil supplied by Goldpetrol Joint Operating Company Inc., 60 percent owned by Singapore-listed Interra Resources Ltd. and 40 percent by North Petroleum International Co. Ltd., a subsidiary of China ZhenHua Oil Co. Ltd., itself a subsidiary of NORINCO, a Chinese state-owned arms conglomerate that also supplies weapons directly to the SAC. Goldpetrol supplied MOGE with more than three million barrels of oil between January 2021 and end of 2024, generating over USD 72 million in revenues for Interra Resources. Interra requested a trading halt on 3 February 2025 and suspended trading on the Singapore Stock Exchange on 6 February 2025, following a joint investigation by Justice for Myanmar and Finance Uncovered into potential sanctions violations. As of April 2026, Singapore Exchange Regulation (SGX RegCo) is still reviewing Interra's trading resumption proposal. MOGE has been designated by the EU and is subject to a US financial services directive but has not been fully sanctioned by the US, UK, Australia, or Canada.

Legal Framework and Sanctions

•The OFAC jet fuel sector determination of 23 August 2023, issued under Executive Order 14014, established that Myanmar's jet fuel sector falls within the scope of US Burma-related sanctions, creating authority to designate any person operating in that sector without requiring proof that fuel reached a military aircraft. Executive Order 14014 was renewed by President Trump on 3 February 2026.
•Canada enacted the most comprehensive government response to date. On 27 October 2023, through amendment SOR/2023-228 to the Special Economic Measures (Burma) Regulations, Canada prohibited the export, sale, supply, and shipment of aviation fuel to Myanmar and the provision of maritime shipping insurance for vessels transporting it. Canada is the only government to have enacted such a measure.
•In April 2024, the UN Human Rights Council adopted Resolution 55/20 calling on member states to refrain from exporting aviation fuel to Myanmar when there are reasonable grounds to suspect military use. Vietnam held a Council seat during the relevant period, did not oppose the resolution, and has since taken no action against Hai Linh Co. Ltd. or the Cai Mep terminal.

Documented Advocacy Outcomes

•Company withdrawals following Deadly Cargo (November 2022): Amnesty International's pre-publication engagement with three companies produced confirmed and durable withdrawals. Thai Oil Public Company Limited suspended Myanmar aviation fuel sales on 14 October 2022, before publication, after being notified of Amnesty International's findings. Its internal due diligence, using the World-Check One database and contractual warranties, had not surfaced the military end-use risk; Amnesty International's disclosure was sufficient to prompt suspension without regulatory compulsion. Wilhelmsen Ships Service Myanmar issued its cessation statement on 18 October 2022, committing to cease providing agency services for any vessel discharging Jet A-1 at Myanmar ports "irrespective of designations regarding end use." Pan Ocean Co. Ltd. committed after publication that its ships would no longer carry aviation fuel to Myanmar. All three have been absent from every subsequent documented shipment across four subsequent Amnesty International investigations.
•Canada's first-mover designations (December 2022) and aviation fuel trade ban (October 2023): Canada was the first government to designate any entity specifically for its role in Myanmar's aviation fuel supply chain, sanctioning Asia Sun Group Company Limited in December 2022. Canada subsequently enacted the most comprehensive legal measure taken by any government. The October 2023 amendment SOR/2023-228 bans the export, sale, supply, and shipment of aviation fuel to Myanmar and the provision of maritime shipping insurance for vessels transporting it. The ban operates independently of individual designation status and does not require proof of military end-use. No other government has enacted a comparable measure.
•The collapse of Singapore as a supply hub: Singapore was the dominant direct-loading jurisdiction for aviation fuel to Myanmar in Phase 1, accounting for five of the eight Deadly Cargo shipments, four at the SRC terminal and one at the adjacent ExxonMobil berth on Jurong Island, including the first documented post-coup shipment in April 2021. Following Deadly Cargo and successive sanctions rounds, Singapore's government launched an investigation into Singapore-based entities supplying arms and related goods to Myanmar's military. Singapore's Foreign Minister issued a statement in February 2023 committing to prohibit the transfer of arms and dual-use goods to Myanmar. Singapore-based companies supplying arms and military equipment to Myanmar dropped from 81 in FY2022 to six in FY2023, a decline of more than 90 percent, according to the UN Special Rapporteur's June 2024 analysis. The SAC's overall procurement of weapons and military goods through the formal banking system fell by one-third, from USD 377 million in FY2022 to USD 253 million in FY2023. Singapore has not appeared as a direct load port in any documented Myanmar aviation fuel shipment since 2024.
•The OFAC jet fuel sector determination (August 2023): Civil society organisations advocated from 2022 for a US legal mechanism allowing designation of supply chain actors without requiring proof that fuel reached a military aircraft. The August 2023 OFAC determination under Executive Order 14014 addressed this gap, creating authority to designate any person operating in Myanmar's jet fuel sector without that end-use threshold. The determination's behavioural effect was measurable. Documented shipments paused between September and November 2023, consistent with supply chain participants reassessing their risk exposure.
•UN Human Rights Council Resolution 55/20 (April 2024): The HRC adopted Resolution 55/20 by consensus on 4 April 2024, calling on all member states to refrain from transferring aviation fuel to Myanmar when there are reasonable grounds to suspect military use. The resolution was cited by the UN Special Rapporteur as part of the basis for his June 2024 Banking on the Death Trade report and reinforces the political framework for additional national trade bans.
•Kasikorn Bank and the June 2023 US bank designations: Kasikorn Bank reduced its Myanmar military procurement facilitation from over USD 35 million in FY2022 to under USD 5 million in the first quarter of FY2023, before the June 2023 US designations of MFTB and MICB. Following those designations, Kasikorn confirmed to the UN Special Rapporteur that it had terminated its nostro account relationships with both institutions. By contrast, Siam Commercial Bank's Myanmar military procurement transactions jumped from under USD 5 million in FY2022 to over USD 100 million in FY2023, illustrating that individual bank action displaces rather than eliminates financial flows without a coordinated regulatory intervention.
•Fair Finance Thailand and the Thai parliamentary inquiry (July 2024): Following publication of the UN Special Rapporteur's Banking on the Death Trade report, Fair Finance Thailand published an open letter to five Thai banks on 17 July 2024, calling for enhanced due diligence and closure of accounts held by Myanmar military-controlled banks. Thai banks subsequently appeared before a parliamentary committee. In December 2024, the Bank of Thailand and the Anti-Money Laundering Office issued a joint policy directive, effective 27 December 2024, requiring all Thai financial institutions to implement enhanced due diligence on high-risk country transactions with mandatory verification of ultimate beneficial ownership.
•Interra Resources SGX suspension (February 2025): Following Justice for Myanmar's and Finance Uncovered's investigation into Interra Resources' crude oil supply to MOGE, Interra suspended trading on the Singapore Stock Exchange on 6 February 2025. As of April 2026, SGX RegCo continues to review Interra's trading resumption proposal.

Accountability Gaps

The following entities hold central, documented roles in the supply chain and have not been sanctioned in any jurisdiction as of April 2026: Myanma Petrochemical Enterprise (MPE); National Energy Prime Aviation Services Co. Ltd. (NEPAS); Myan Oil Terminal Company Limited; United Amara Bank (UAB); CB Energy; Tawan-Oil Trading; Hai Linh Co. Ltd.; BARAAWE 1 (formerly HUITONG 78) and its owner Abu Marine Co. Ltd. and manager Pacific Light Operation and Ship Management; and LS MERCURY (formerly YONG SHENG 56) and its owner Legendsea Co. Ltd. and manager Allsea Management JSC.

The 23 August 2023 OFAC sector determination provides the legal authority to designate all of the above without additional evidentiary steps. The UN Special Rapporteur's June 2024 Figure 7 transaction table provides a documented evidentiary basis for CB Energy, Tawan-Oil Trading, and UAB designation submissions. The Amnesty International January 2026 and Reuters January 2026 investigations provide the documented basis for the ghost fleet vessel and maritime entity submissions.

In February 2026, Justice for Myanmar formally called on the US, UK, EU, Canada, and Australia to impose coordinated designations on MPE, Hai Linh Co. Ltd., the four ghost fleet vessels, and their owners and managers. As of April 2026, none have been designated.
