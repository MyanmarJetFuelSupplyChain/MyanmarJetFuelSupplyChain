Puma Energy Holdings Pte. Ltd. & Trafigura Group Pte. Ltd.
Profile 5.1
Summary Assessment
Puma Energy Holdings Pte. Ltd. and its majority shareholder Trafigura Group Pte. Ltd. were, from 2013 to December 2022, the dominant foreign entities in Myanmar's aviation fuel supply chain. Puma Energy became Myanmar's first foreign aviation fuel importer, building the Puma Energy Asia Sun (PEAS) import terminal at Thilawa port and holding a minority beneficial stake in National Energy Prime Aviation Services Company Limited (NEPAS), Myanmar's sole aviation fuel distributor. Together PEAS and NEPAS made Puma Energy the most important importer and distributor of aviation fuel in Myanmar from 2015 until its exit announcement in October 2022 and the completion of the asset sale in December 2022. Puma Energy is Singapore-registered and Geneva-based; Trafigura, which owned approximately 92% of Puma Energy, fully consolidated it into the Trafigura Group in September 2021. Following the February 2021 military coup, armed soldiers entered and remained at the PEAS terminal. Puma Energy acknowledged that despite controls it put in place, the military forcibly obtained fuel at NEPAS airports. A confidential Human Rights Impact Assessment it commissioned in early 2022 concluded that full exit was the only viable option. Puma Energy announced its exit on 5 October 2022 and completed the asset sale in December 2022, transferring PEAS and its NEPAS stake to what it described only as "a locally owned private company," later identified as the Asia Sun Group / Shoon Energy network. Amnesty International assessed this exit as insufficiently transparent and the human rights assurances obtained from the buyer as essentially meaningless. Ten documented shipments arrived at the PEAS terminal during the period April 2021 through December 2022. Neither Puma Energy nor Trafigura has been sanctioned by any government in connection with their Myanmar activities.

1
Role in the Aviation Fuel Supply Chain
Pre-coup position: market dominance

Puma Energy became the most important importer and distributor of aviation fuel in Myanmar from 2015 to October 2022. Its position rested on two interlocking assets: the PEAS terminal at Thilawa port and its stake in NEPAS. The PEAS terminal, constructed after Puma Energy won a tender in June 2013 to lease Plot 3 of the Thilawa area port, was the primary entry point for imported Jet A-1 into Myanmar. NEPAS, a joint venture between Puma Energy (via Puma Energy Irrawaddy Aviation Pte. Ltd., now PEIA Pte. Ltd., holding a 49% beneficial stake) and MPE (51%), held the sole aviation fuel import licence and operated storage and refuelling facilities at 11 airports. In 2018 Asia Sun Aviation Pte. Ltd. (subsequently Shoon Energy Pte. Ltd.) acquired 30% of the PEIA vehicle, giving the Asia Sun Group an approximately 16% indirect beneficial interest in NEPAS; NEPAS itself remained a two-party structure between PEIA and MPE throughout. Puma Energy acknowledged it supplied aviation fuel to Myanmar's military before the coup, confirmed by two sources close to the company, three former members of the air force, and its own Aviation Business brochure. (AI Nov 2022)

Post-coup role and attempted controls

Amnesty International found that Puma Energy played a role "in almost every step of the supply chain" of aviation fuel to the Myanmar military after the coup: acting as consignee for imports; managing the PEAS terminal; paying for transport to NEPAS storage facilities; and managing NEPAS storage facilities, several of which supply the military. On the day of the coup, 1 February 2021, armed soldiers entered the PEAS terminal and remained there. Puma Energy briefly suspended operations on 10 February 2021 before resuming. After the coup, Puma Energy stated it had "not supplied, sold or distributed any fuel or products to the Myanmar Air Force" and that it had "exercised its influence so that NEPAS terminated supply to the MAF," thereby confirming that supply to the air force had occurred before the coup. Puma Energy acknowledged that despite controls designed to segregate civilian and military supply, "the military was forcibly demanding fuel at selected NEPAS airport facilities." (AI Nov 2022)

A confidential Human Rights Impact Assessment commissioned by Puma Energy in early 2022 concluded: first, that Puma Energy had taken positive steps to protect employee safety and leveraged its influence to limit supply to civilian airlines; second, that it would prove increasingly difficult to continue to exert even limited influence on MPE and maintain those controls; and third, that the military was forcibly demanding fuel at NEPAS airports. The Board's conclusion, on the basis of this assessment, was that full exit was the only option. (AI Nov 2022)

Documented shipments during Puma Energy's period of control

Ten shipments of aviation fuel arrived at the PEAS terminal at Thilawa during the period of Puma Energy's ownership and management (April 2021 to December 2022). Eight arrived while Puma Energy remained the undisputed operator. Two further shipments (October and December 2022) arrived during the brief transition period after the exit agreement was signed but before the sale was completed. Load ports in this period included: Singapore Refining Co. (SRC) on Jurong Island (four shipments in April and June 2021, December 2021 and February 2022); the Vadinar Oil Terminal, Gujarat, India (December 2021); the Thai Oil Marine Terminal, Sriracha, Thailand (June 2022); ExxonMobil's berth on Jurong Island, Singapore (June 2022); Pengerang Independent Terminals, Malaysia (August 2022); Bangchak Oil Refinery, Bangkok Port (October 2022); and a Reliance Industries terminal at Sikka, India (December 2022). Confirmed suppliers in this period include Singapore Petroleum Company / PetroChina, Chevron, Thai Oil, and Rosneft's supply chain via Nayara Energy / Vadinar. For cross-reference with all documented shipments, see the master shipment table. (AI Nov 2022; AI Mar 2023)

Trafigura's board representation in NEPAS post-coup

Following the coup, Puma Energy's two NEPAS board representatives were replaced by Trafigura employees. Mikhail Solomon Zeldovich (Trafigura Pte., Director for Mongolia and Vietnam) joined the NEPAS board on 2 August 2021. Timothy John Codrington (Trafigura Pte., Head of Business Development Asia) joined on 15 March 2022. This places Trafigura, as majority owner of Puma Energy and direct supplier of NEPAS board members, directly in the NEPAS governance structure throughout the period in which imported aviation fuel was reaching Myanmar's military. Trafigura is also a preferred supplier of petroleum products to Puma Energy; Puma Energy in turn is one of Trafigura's largest midstream services clients. (AI Nov 2022)

Exit and asset transfer

Puma Energy announced on 5 October 2022 that it had "entered into agreements to fully exit Myanmar," identifying the buyer only as "a locally owned private company." Puma Energy stated it had secured "undertakings from the investor that it would comply with Human Rights laws, implement relevant policies and procedures, as well as not use the assets or services to commit human rights violations." No timing or details of the transaction were disclosed. The sale was completed in December 2022. The buyer was subsequently identified by Amnesty International, Global Witness and Burma Campaign UK as the Asia Sun Group / Shoon Energy network, which had pre-existing documented involvement in military aviation fuel supply. Amnesty International assessed the human rights assurances obtained from the buyer as "essentially meaningless" given this background, and characterised Puma Energy's overall exit as insufficiently transparent. (AI Nov 2022; AI Mar 2023)

The responsible exit standard: Under the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights and the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises, in cases of asset sale a company must assess the human rights capability of the buyer and request, through contractual provisions, that the buyer put specific human rights-related policies and procedures in place. Amnesty International assessed that Puma Energy's exit failed this standard: it did not publicly disclose the buyer's identity, did not provide the public with detailed information on disengagement, and did not meaningfully engage with representatives of Myanmar communities affected by air strikes to find appropriate measures of reparation. AI also called on the Swiss government to investigate Puma Energy's role in supplying aviation fuel to the Myanmar military from 2015 to the date of departure. (AI Nov 2022)

Key Relationships
•National Energy Prime Aviation Services Company Limited (NEPAS): Puma Energy held a 49% beneficial stake in NEPAS through Puma Energy Irrawaddy Aviation Pte. Ltd. (subsequently PEIA Pte. Ltd.) from NEPAS's establishment. In 2018, Puma Energy sold 30% of the PEIA vehicle to Asia Sun Aviation (subsequently Shoon Energy Pte. Ltd.), giving Asia Sun Group an approximately 16% indirect beneficial interest in NEPAS. The remaining 70% of PEIA and the full NEPAS stake passed to the Shoon Group in December 2022. NEPAS is unsanctioned in all jurisdictions. See Profile 1.2. (AI Nov 2022)
•Puma Energy Asia Sun Company Limited (PEAS) / Myan Oil Terminal Company Limited: The import terminal at Thilawa port, Yangon, originally constructed and operated by Puma Energy. Armed soldiers entered the terminal on the day of the coup, 1 February 2021, and remained there. The terminal passed to the Shoon Group in December 2022, was renamed Shoon Energy Thilawa Terminal Company Limited, and subsequently Myan Oil Terminal Company Limited. Puma Energy Irrawaddy Pte. Ltd. (subsequently P.E.I Energy Pte. Ltd.) was the Singapore holding company for this asset. See Profile 1.3 and Joint Profile 2.1. (AI Nov 2022; AI Mar 2023)
•Myanma Petrochemical Enterprise (MPE): The junta-controlled 51% partner in NEPAS. MPE was Puma Energy's joint venture partner throughout the NEPAS operation. MPE co-controls the Thilawa terminal following Puma Energy's exit. MPE is unsanctioned in all jurisdictions. See Profile 1.1. (AI Nov 2022)
•Asia Sun Group / Shoon Energy Pte. Ltd.: The Asia Sun Group, through Asia Sun Aviation Pte. Ltd. (subsequently Shoon Energy Pte. Ltd.), acquired a 30% stake in Puma Energy Irrawaddy Aviation in 2018, giving it an approximately 16% indirect NEPAS beneficial interest and establishing the Singapore holding structure that Khin Phyu Win subsequently consolidated. The Asia Sun / Shoon Group was the undisclosed buyer of Puma Energy's entire Myanmar portfolio in December 2022. Asia Sun Group is sanctioned by the US, UK, EU, Canada and Australia. See Profile 1.4 and Joint Profile 2.1. (AI Nov 2022; AI Mar 2023)
•Nayara Energy / Vadinar Oil Terminal (row 3 nexus): Trafigura held a 49.13% stake in Nayara Energy alongside Rosneft Singapore Pte. Ltd. (49.13%), the owner of the Vadinar Oil Terminal in Gujarat, India. The Vadinar terminal was the load port for the December 2021 Swarna Mala shipment (row 3, 15,710 MT). Trafigura and Nayara Energy both stated they were not involved in that specific shipment's jet fuel sale. Trafigura planned to sell its Nayara Energy stake in the first half of 2022; as of September 2022 the sale had not been confirmed. (AI Nov 2022)
•Indian Oil Corporation Limited (IOCL): India's state-owned IOCL sent approximately USD 140 million in jet fuel to Puma Energy Supply and Trading in Singapore and its parent Trafigura from 2017 to 2020, before the coup. This confirms IOCL as one of the upstream fuel suppliers to Puma Energy's Myanmar operations in the pre-coup period. JFM noted: "Before its exit in 2022, Puma was Myanmar military's main source of jet fuel." (JFM Mar 2024)
•Wilhelmsen Ships Service Myanmar Ltd.: Wilhelmsen acted as shipping agent for a number of Jet A-1 shipments during the Puma Energy period, confirmed by Wilhelmsen itself and leaked corporate documents. Between October 2020 and June 2021, Wilhelmsen Myanmar received at least MMK 15.7 million (approximately USD 7,500) to manage NEPAS and Puma Energy shipments. Following the publication of Amnesty International's November 2022 report, Wilhelmsen confirmed it would immediately cease providing agency services for vessels discharging Jet A-1 at Myanmar ports. See Profile 6.9. (AI Nov 2022)

4
Advocacy Interventions and Outcomes
•2 September 2022. Amnesty International meets in person with Omar Zaafrani, Puma Energy's Head of ESG and Corporate Affairs, in Geneva, as part of its pre-publication engagement presenting the findings of the Deadly Cargo investigation to Puma Energy and seeking comment. Amnesty International also engaged Puma Energy in subsequent correspondence. Puma Energy subsequently provided written responses addressing controls, exit, and the responsible withdrawal standard. (AI Nov 2022)
•5 October 2022. Puma Energy announces its Myanmar exit approximately one month after Amnesty International's in-person presentation of findings. The exit announcement is a single sentence identifying the buyer only as "a locally owned private company." AI notes that the Board had taken the decision to exit "earlier this year" following the Human Rights Impact Assessment, but the public announcement follows directly from AI's pre-publication engagement. (AI Nov 2022)
•October 2022. Following Amnesty International's pre-publication outreach, three companies confirm they will stop shipping aviation fuel to Myanmar before the report is published. Thai Oil confirms it will put on hold any further shipments. Pan Ocean confirms it will no longer allow its vessels to transport aviation fuel to Myanmar. Wilhelmsen confirms it will immediately cease providing agency services to vessels discharging Jet A-1 at Myanmar ports. These pre-publication commitments represent the most significant immediate advocacy outcomes of the Deadly Cargo investigation. (AI Nov 2022; AI Mar 2023)
•3 November 2022. Amnesty International's Deadly Cargo report is published, documenting Puma Energy's central role in the post-coup aviation fuel supply chain, including its pre-coup supply to the military, post-coup awareness that fuel was reaching the military despite stated controls, and the opaque nature of its exit. AI calls on Puma Energy to contribute to remediation through consultations with affected communities, and calls on the Swiss government to investigate Puma Energy's role in supplying aviation fuel to the Myanmar military. Puma Energy's asset sale is completed in December 2022. (AI Nov 2022)
•1 March 2023. Amnesty International, Global Witness and Burma Campaign UK publish their follow-up report identifying Shoon Energy as the buyer of Puma Energy's Myanmar assets. The report documents two additional shipments that arrived at the former PEAS terminal while Puma Energy retained control (rows 9 and 10: Big Sea 104 in October 2022 and Prime V in December 2022). The report assesses the human rights assurances obtained from the buyer as essentially meaningless given the Asia Sun Group's documented military supply role, and characterises the exit as insufficiently transparent. (AI Mar 2023)
5
Recommended Actions
•Call on the Swiss government to investigate Puma Energy's role in supplying aviation fuel to the Myanmar military: Amnesty International explicitly called on the Government of Switzerland to investigate Puma Energy over its role in the supply of aviation fuel to the Myanmar military between 2015 and the date of its departure. Switzerland is not part of the Myanmar sanctions coalition but has domestic legal frameworks for corporate accountability. Civil society should formally transmit the AI Nov 2022 evidentiary package to the Swiss Federal Department of Justice and Police and the State Secretariat for Economic Affairs, requesting a determination on whether Puma Energy's conduct warrants investigation under Swiss law. The fact that Puma Energy senior leadership operated out of Geneva and that AI met with its Head of ESG and Corporate Affairs there provides a clear Swiss jurisdictional anchor. (AI Nov 2022)
•Seek transparency on the terms of the Puma Energy exit and any outstanding remediation obligations: Puma Energy's exit was announced in a single sentence and completed without public disclosure of the buyer, the terms of the transaction, any human rights provisions in the sale agreement, or any engagement with Myanmar communities affected by air strikes. Under the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights, companies that have contributed to harm are required to contribute to remediation. AI called on Puma Energy to begin consultations with representatives of Myanmar communities affected by air strikes. Civil society and investor networks should engage Trafigura, as Puma Energy's majority owner, to confirm what remediation steps, if any, Puma Energy undertook or committed to before or after the exit, and to make the contractual terms of the buyer's human rights undertakings publicly available. (AI Nov 2022)
•Engage Trafigura directly on its board representation in NEPAS and its role as Puma Energy's majority owner: Trafigura provided two employees to the NEPAS board during the period in which imported aviation fuel was reaching Myanmar's military (August 2021 to at least March 2022). As the entity that held approximately 92% beneficial ownership of Puma Energy and fully consolidated it into the Trafigura Group in September 2021, Trafigura bore the ultimate beneficial ownership responsibility for Puma Energy's Myanmar operations throughout the post-coup period. Civil society investors in Trafigura and its associated financial counterparties should engage the company on what oversight it exercised over Puma Energy's Myanmar operations, what instructions if any it gave to its NEPAS board representatives, and whether it has conducted or published any human rights impact assessment of its own Myanmar exposure. (AI Nov 2022)
•Maintain monitoring of Puma Energy's and Trafigura's future aviation fuel activities in high-risk jurisdictions: The documented failure of Puma Energy's controls in Myanmar, where the military forcibly obtained fuel at NEPAS airports despite stated segregation measures, is directly relevant to any future Puma Energy or Trafigura operations in jurisdictions where military end-use diversion is a foreseeable risk. Civil society should track Puma Energy's and Trafigura's activities in similar market contexts, referencing the Myanmar precedent as establishing that their existing compliance systems are insufficient to prevent military diversion in contexts where the military can demand fuel directly. (AI Nov 2022)
