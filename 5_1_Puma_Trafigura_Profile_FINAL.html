Puma Energy Holdings Pte. Ltd. & Trafigura Group Pte. Ltd.
Profile 5.1
Summary Assessment
Puma Energy Holdings Pte. Ltd., majority-owned by Trafigura, built and operated the Thilawa import terminal and held a beneficial stake in NEPAS from 2013 until December 2022, making it the dominant foreign entity in Myanmar's aviation fuel supply chain before and after the coup. Armed soldiers entered the terminal on the morning of the coup and remained. A confidential Human Rights Impact Assessment commissioned in early 2022 concluded that full exit was the only viable option after military personnel began forcibly demanding fuel at NEPAS airport facilities. Puma announced its exit on 5 October 2022 and completed the asset sale in December 2022, transferring PEAS and its NEPAS stake to what it described only as "a locally owned private company," later identified as the Asia Sun Group. Amnesty International assessed the exit as insufficiently transparent and the human rights assurances obtained from the buyer as essentially meaningless. Ten shipments arrived at the terminal during Puma's period of control. Neither Puma Energy nor Trafigura has been sanctioned.

1
Role in the Aviation Fuel Supply Chain

Puma Energy's position rested on two assets. The PEAS terminal at Thilawa, built after Puma won the lease in June 2013, was the primary entry point for imported Jet A-1. NEPAS, a joint venture between Puma Energy (via PEIA Pte. Ltd., holding a 49 percent beneficial stake) and MPE (51 percent), held the sole aviation fuel import licence and managed eleven airport facilities. Puma acknowledged it supplied aviation fuel to Myanmar's military before the coup. After the coup, it stated it had exercised its influence to terminate supply to the air force, thereby confirming that pre-coup supply had occurred. Despite post-coup controls, the military forcibly demanded fuel at NEPAS airport facilities. Amnesty International found Puma played a role "in almost every step of the supply chain" to the military after the coup. (AI Nov 2022)

After the coup, Trafigura placed two of its own employees on the NEPAS board: Mikhail Solomon Zeldovich (2 August 2021) and Timothy John Codrington (15 March 2022). This places Trafigura directly in the NEPAS governance structure throughout the period in which imported aviation fuel was reaching Myanmar's military. Trafigura fully consolidated Puma Energy into the Trafigura Group in September 2021. (AI Nov 2022)

Ten shipments arrived at the PEAS terminal during Puma's period of control (April 2021 to December 2022). Load ports included the SRC terminal on Jurong Island (four shipments), Vadinar in India, Thai Oil's Sriracha terminal, ExxonMobil's Jurong Island berth, Pengerang in Malaysia, Bangchak's Bangkok Port terminal, and Reliance Industries' Sikka terminal. Two further shipments arrived during the transition period after the exit agreement was signed but before the sale completed. (AI Nov 2022; AI Mar 2023)

Puma announced its exit on 5 October 2022, identifying the buyer only as "a locally owned private company." The buyer was later identified as the Asia Sun Group. The UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights require a company in asset sale to assess the human rights capability of the buyer and seek contractual human rights commitments. Amnesty International assessed Puma's exit failed this standard: it did not disclose the buyer publicly, did not engage affected communities, and did not seek adequate reparations. (AI Nov 2022; AI Mar 2023)

Key Relationships
•National Energy Prime Aviation Services Company Limited (NEPAS): Puma Energy held a 49% beneficial stake in NEPAS through Puma Energy Irrawaddy Aviation Pte. Ltd. (subsequently PEIA Pte. Ltd.) from NEPAS's establishment. In 2018, Puma Energy sold 30% of the PEIA vehicle to Asia Sun Aviation (subsequently Shoon Energy Pte. Ltd.), giving Asia Sun Group an approximately 16% indirect beneficial interest in NEPAS. The remaining 70% of PEIA and the full NEPAS stake passed to the Shoon Group in December 2022. NEPAS is unsanctioned in all jurisdictions. See Profile 1.2. (AI Nov 2022)
•Puma Energy Asia Sun Company Limited (PEAS) / Myan Oil Terminal Company Limited: The import terminal at Thilawa port, Yangon, originally constructed and operated by Puma Energy. Armed soldiers entered the terminal on the day of the coup, 1 February 2021, and remained there. The terminal passed to the Shoon Group in December 2022, was renamed Shoon Energy Thilawa Terminal Company Limited, and subsequently Myan Oil Terminal Company Limited. Puma Energy Irrawaddy Pte. Ltd. (subsequently P.E.I Energy Pte. Ltd.) was the Singapore holding company for this asset. See Profile 1.3 and Joint Profile 2.1. (AI Nov 2022; AI Mar 2023)
•Myanma Petrochemical Enterprise (MPE): The junta-controlled 51% partner in NEPAS. MPE was Puma Energy's joint venture partner throughout the NEPAS operation. MPE co-controls the Thilawa terminal following Puma Energy's exit. MPE is unsanctioned in all jurisdictions. See Profile 1.1. (AI Nov 2022)
•Asia Sun Group / Shoon Energy Pte. Ltd.: The Asia Sun Group, through Asia Sun Aviation Pte. Ltd. (subsequently Shoon Energy Pte. Ltd.), acquired a 30% stake in Puma Energy Irrawaddy Aviation in 2018, giving it an approximately 16% indirect NEPAS beneficial interest and establishing the Singapore holding structure that Khin Phyu Win subsequently consolidated. The Asia Sun / Shoon Group was the undisclosed buyer of Puma Energy's entire Myanmar portfolio in December 2022. Asia Sun Group is sanctioned by the US, UK, EU, Canada and Australia. See Profile 1.4 and Joint Profile 2.1. (AI Nov 2022; AI Mar 2023)
•Nayara Energy / Vadinar Oil Terminal (row 3 nexus): Trafigura held a 49.13% stake in Nayara Energy alongside Rosneft Singapore Pte. Ltd. (49.13%), the owner of the Vadinar Oil Terminal in Gujarat, India. The Vadinar terminal was the load port for the December 2021 Swarna Mala shipment (row 3, 15,710 MT). Trafigura and Nayara Energy both stated they were not involved in that specific shipment's jet fuel sale. Trafigura planned to sell its Nayara Energy stake in the first half of 2022; as of September 2022 the sale had not been confirmed. (AI Nov 2022)
•Indian Oil Corporation Limited (IOCL): India's state-owned IOCL sent approximately USD 140 million in jet fuel to Puma Energy Supply and Trading in Singapore and its parent Trafigura from 2017 to 2020, before the coup. This confirms IOCL as one of the upstream fuel suppliers to Puma Energy's Myanmar operations in the pre-coup period. JFM noted: "Before its exit in 2022, Puma was Myanmar military's main source of jet fuel." (JFM Mar 2024)
•Wilhelmsen Ships Service Myanmar Ltd.: Wilhelmsen acted as shipping agent for a number of Jet A-1 shipments during the Puma Energy period, confirmed by Wilhelmsen itself and leaked corporate documents. Between October 2020 and June 2021, Wilhelmsen Myanmar received at least MMK 15.7 million (approximately USD 7,500) to manage NEPAS and Puma Energy shipments. Following the publication of Amnesty International's November 2022 report, Wilhelmsen confirmed it would immediately cease providing agency services for vessels discharging Jet A-1 at Myanmar ports. See Profile 6.9. (AI Nov 2022)

Advocacy Interventions and Outcomes
•2 September 2022. Amnesty International meets with Puma Energy's Head of ESG in Geneva to present Deadly Cargo findings before publication. (AI Nov 2022)
•5 October 2022. Puma Energy announces its Myanmar exit. The announcement is a single sentence. The buyer is not identified. (AI Nov 2022)
•October 2022. Three companies commit to cease involvement before Deadly Cargo is published: Thai Oil suspends aviation fuel shipments; Pan Ocean commits its vessels will no longer carry aviation fuel to Myanmar; Wilhelmsen ceases agency services for vessels discharging Jet A-1 at Myanmar ports. These are the most significant immediate outcomes of the Deadly Cargo investigation. (AI Nov 2022; AI Mar 2023)
•3 November 2022. Deadly Cargo is published. Amnesty International calls on Puma Energy to consult affected communities and on Switzerland to investigate Puma Energy's role. The asset sale completes in December 2022. (AI Nov 2022)
•1 March 2023. Amnesty International, Global Witness, and Burma Campaign UK identify Shoon Energy as the buyer, document two additional shipments during the transition period, and assess the exit as insufficiently transparent and the buyer's human rights assurances as essentially meaningless. (AI Mar 2023)
5
Recommended Actions
•Call on Switzerland to investigate Puma Energy's role. Amnesty International explicitly called on the Swiss government to investigate Puma Energy's supply of aviation fuel to Myanmar's military from 2015 to its departure. Puma Energy's senior leadership operated from Geneva; Amnesty International met with its Head of ESG there. Civil society should transmit the AI Nov 2022 evidence package to the Swiss Federal Department of Justice and Police and the State Secretariat for Economic Affairs. (AI Nov 2022)
•Engage Trafigura on its NEPAS board role and remediation obligations. Trafigura placed two employees on the NEPAS board after the coup and held approximately 92 percent of Puma Energy throughout. Investors and civil society should engage Trafigura to confirm what oversight it exercised over Puma Energy's Myanmar operations, what instructions if any it gave to its NEPAS board representatives, and whether it has assessed its own exposure under the UN Guiding Principles. Trafigura should also be asked to confirm whether any remediation commitments were made to affected communities as part of the exit. (AI Nov 2022)
•Seek disclosure of the exit terms and buyer commitments. Puma Energy's buyer undertakings on human rights were never made public. Under the UN Guiding Principles, companies that have contributed to harm must contribute to remediation. Trafigura and Puma Energy should be pressed to make the contractual terms of those undertakings publicly available and to confirm what engagement, if any, took place with communities affected by air strikes from the bases that received fuel through the NEPAS distribution chain. (AI Nov 2022; AI Mar 2023)
